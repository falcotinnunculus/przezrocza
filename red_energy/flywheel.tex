\begin{frame}{Why flywheels are cool}
    \begin{columns}
        \column{0.5\textwidth}
% \section*{4. Advantages of Flywheel Energy Storage}
Advantages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{High Efficiency} %Flywheels typically achieve an efficiency of 85--90\%, making them highly effective at storing and retrieving energy.
    \item \textbf{Durability} %Minimal degradation over time compared to chemical batteries, allowing for a long operational life.
    \item \textbf{Fast Response Time} %Ideal for applications requiring rapid energy delivery, such as grid frequency regulation or peak shaving.
    \item \textbf{Environmentally Friendly} %No toxic chemicals or hazardous materials involved, making disposal and recycling easier.
    \item \textbf{High Power Density} %Capable of delivering significant amounts of power in short time frames.
\end{itemize}
        \column{0.5\textwidth}
% \section*{5. Challenges and Limitations}
Disadvantages:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Self-Discharge} %Energy is lost due to friction in bearings and air resistance, leading to reduced long-term storage capacity.
    \item \textbf{Material Constraints} %Flywheels must withstand extremely high stresses at high rotational speeds, requiring advanced materials like carbon fiber composites.
    \item \textbf{High Initial Cost} %The upfront costs of manufacturing and installing flywheel systems are often higher than those of chemical batteries.
    \item \textbf{Low Energy Density} %While power density is high, energy density is relatively low compared to systems like lithium-ion batteries.
    \item \textbf{Complexity of Bearings} %Advanced bearings, such as magnetic or superconducting bearings, add to system complexity and maintenance requirements.
\end{itemize}
    \end{columns}
\end{frame}

    rotational energy: \(E_{kin}=\displaystyle{\frac12} J \omega^2\)

    \item \(K\) -- shape factor [dimensionless]

    \item \(\sigma\) -- tensile strength [Pa]
